# Compass Book visual-question audit

## Decision rule

Use an image when the difficult cognitive step is **imagining a situation**. Use an interaction when the difficult step is **comparing, arranging, or trading off known things**. Keep reflection text-led when the difficult step is **retrieving personal evidence**.

Images are prompts for recognition, not personality tests and not decoration. Every visual question should follow this sequence:

1. See a concrete situation.
2. Notice attention, attraction, resistance, or relief.
3. Select without needing to justify it.
4. Optionally name what mattered.
5. Treat the result as a clue to test, not a verdict.

Every recognition question needs an honest escape such as `I don't know yet`, `None of these`, or `Show me different scenes`. An escape is a complete answer and must not reduce rewards, completion, or dignity.

## Prefer interaction over generated imagery

- Chapter I scores, good-enough levels, minimum-safe levels, momentum, and spillover: strengthen the live wheel and comparison feedback.
- Chapter IV Island 61 interests: use broad selection, preference ranking, time ranking, and a final 3–5 lock-in celebration.
- Chapter V quest comparison and portfolio: use forge trays, weighted tokens, costs, and side-by-side quest cards.
- Chapter VI Start Engine and Minimum Mode: use a buildable mechanism or cockpit with the player's own language.

These questions already concern things the player can name; the challenge is prioritisation or system-building, not imagination.

## Keep evidence-led and mostly text-first

- Chapter II values, needs, overshoot, shadow/drift, counterbalance, and boundary evidence.
- Chapter I remembered moments and counterevidence.
- Chapter IV evidence of what others rely on, practical value, access, and willingness for the real process.
- Chapter V motives, recognition test, accepted cost, and counterevidence.

Stock scenes here risk priming a socially desirable answer or replacing memory with identification. Helpful aids should be episode prompts, timelines, examples that rotate, and `I can't recall yet` escapes—not identity imagery.

## Reusable visual-question pattern

1. A neutral instruction: `Look first; interpret second.`
2. Four to eight concrete cards per comparison set; avoid exhausting long grids.
3. Visible scene title and one-sentence description.
4. Abstract category shown as a secondary theme, after the scene.
5. `I don't know yet` plus, when appropriate, `None of these` and `Show me different scenes`.
6. Selection feedback that names a clue, never a type: `This may be worth testing.`
7. Optional evidence prompt: `What part of the scene pulled you?`
8. Full keyboard semantics, selected state, descriptive alt text, and a text-only fallback.

## Fun without manipulation

- Give the interaction a discovery metaphor: telescope, lantern, field notes, constellation, forge, or compass bearing.
- Celebrate completion of observation, not conformity or certainty.
- Reveal a short pattern summary only after enough observations, and make its evidence inspectable.
- Let the player revise answers freely; uncertainty and contradiction are useful data.
- Use small sensory rewards—light, sound, material motion—without streak loss, false scarcity, shame, or reward penalties.

## Production gate for the next visual set

Before generating another sprite, founder-play the Island 62 reference and answer:

- Did a scene cause recognition faster than the old label list?
- Did any art feel like the expected or morally correct choice?
- Was `I don't know yet` emotionally safe and mechanically complete?
- Did the caption clarify the image without overexplaining it?
- Did the optional evidence prompt produce a more specific answer?

Generate the Living Horizon day-fragment set next only if the reference passes those checks. This prevents a large art rollout from locking in the wrong cognitive pattern.
